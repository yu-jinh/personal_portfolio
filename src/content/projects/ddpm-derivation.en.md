## Summary

This note derives the DDPM training loss step by step, from the forward process to the noise-prediction objective. My own DDPM, trained on Fashion-MNIST for about 10 minutes on a Colab GPU, turns pure noise into Fashion-MNIST images.

A diffusion model must learn to undo noise one step at a time. The true denoising step is intractable on its own.

Three results make it tractable. The forward process has a closed form, so any noise level can be sampled in one step. Conditioned on the clean image, each reverse step is a Gaussian with known mean and variance. A variational bound then splits the objective into per-step terms that compare Gaussians.

The DDPM paper's ablation on unconditional CIFAR-10 supports the final loss. Noise prediction with the simplified loss reaches an FID of 3.17, against 13.22 for the next-best objective.

### Diffusion as a generative model

Probabilistic models have long traded **tractability** against **flexibility**. Tractable models, such as a Gaussian, are easy to evaluate and fit, but they cannot capture the structure of rich datasets. Flexible models can fit almost any structure, but they are expensive to evaluate, train or sample from.

Sohl-Dickstein et al. sidestepped this trade-off. A diffusion process slowly **destroys** the structure in the data, and a model **learns the reverse process** that restores it. Each reverse step is a simple, tractable distribution, yet a chain of many steps is highly flexible.

The 2015 paper did not show high-quality images. That was left to DDPM.

### DDPM

Ho et al. showed that diffusion models **can** generate high-quality images, sometimes beating other generative models of the time. Their key simplifications were:

- A fixed, **linearly increasing** noise schedule $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$
- Gaussian noise only at every step, so every distribution in the derivation stays Gaussian
- A reparameterization in which the network **predicts the noise** $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ rather than the denoised mean

## The forward process

The goal is to get $\mathbf{x}_t$ directly from $\mathbf{x}_0$, without simulating every step in between.

<figure class="figure-media">
<img src="./images/projects/ddpm/markov-chain.svg" alt="Markov chain from x0 to xT: forward arrows q(x_t | x_t-1) add noise, dashed reverse arrows p_theta(x_t-1 | x_t) remove it" loading="lazy">
<figcaption>The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise until only noise remains. The model learns the reverse direction, shown by the dashed arrows.</figcaption>
</figure>

The forward process adds a little noise to the image over and over, until it is indistinguishable from standard Gaussian noise. Writing $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$, each step is:

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\big)
$$

Equivalently, with fresh noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ at every step:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t
$$

Applying this recursion $t$ times works, but it is slow. To find a closed form, substitute $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ once:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}\big) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{\alpha_t-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t
\end{aligned}
$$

The last two terms are independent zero-mean Gaussians:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{\alpha_t-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{0}, (\alpha_t-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1})\mathbf{I}\big) \\
\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{0}, (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

A sum of independent Gaussians is itself Gaussian, and its variance is the sum of their variances: $(\alpha_t - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. So the two noise terms merge into a single $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
$$

Repeating this all the way down to $\mathbf{x}_0$ and writing $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}\cdots\alpha_1}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}\cdots\alpha_1}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \\
&= \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
\end{aligned}
$$

In other words, any intermediate state can be sampled in **one step**:

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\big)
$$

That fully specifies the forward process. Next comes the reverse process, which is what the model has to learn.

## The reverse process

The goal is the distribution of one denoising step, $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$.

On its own this is intractable, but **conditioned on** $\mathbf{x}_0$ it is not. Bayes' theorem rewrites it in terms of forward distributions we already know:

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_0)\, q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}
$$

By the Markov property and the forward-process results, all three terms are Gaussian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_0) &= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\big) \\
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0) &= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\ \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})\mathbf{I}\big) \\
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) &= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

Plugging in the Gaussian densities and keeping only the terms that depend on $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)
&\propto \exp\Bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1})^2}{1-\alpha_t} + \frac{(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_0)^2}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \\
&\hphantom{\propto \exp\Bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(} - \frac{(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0)^2}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\bigg)\Bigg) \\
&= \exp\Bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Big(\frac{\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\Big)\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^2 \\
&\hphantom{= \exp\Bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(} - \Big(\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{1-\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_0\Big)\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + C(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)\bigg)\Bigg)
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a Gaussian in $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$. To read off its mean and variance, compare it with the expanded form of a Gaussian density:

$$
\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}x^2 - \frac{2\mu}{\sigma^2}x + \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2}\right)\right)
$$

Matching the $x^2$ and $x$ coefficients gives:

$$
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}_t^2} = \dfrac{\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t} + \dfrac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \\[2ex]
\dfrac{2\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)}{\tilde{\sigma}_t^2} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{1-\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_t + \dfrac{2\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_0
\end{cases}
$$

Solving for both, then eliminating $\mathbf{x}_0$ with $\mathbf{x}_0 = \big(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\big)/\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}$ from the forward process, gives:

$$
\begin{cases}
\tilde{\sigma}_t^2 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha_t)(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \\[2ex]
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \dfrac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)
\end{cases}
$$

The variance depends only on the fixed schedule. The mean depends on $\mathbf{x}_t$, which we have, and on the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, which we don't. **Predicting that noise is exactly what the network will learn to do.**

## The training objective

### KL divergence

To make the model's distribution match the data, we need a way to compare two distributions. The **KL divergence** does this, and it is never negative. For densities $f$ and $g$, using $\log y \le y - 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(f \,\|\, g) &= \int f(x) \log\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\,dx \\
&= -\int f(x) \log\frac{g(x)}{f(x)}\,dx \\
&\ge -\int f(x) \left(\frac{g(x)}{f(x)} - 1\right) dx \\
&= -\int \big(g(x) - f(x)\big)\,dx = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

### From KL to cross-entropy

We want the model's distribution $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ to be as close as possible to the data distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_0)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\big) &= \int q(\mathbf{x}_0) \log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)}\,d\mathbf{x}_0 \\
&= \int q(\mathbf{x}_0) \log q(\mathbf{x}_0)\,d\mathbf{x}_0 - \int q(\mathbf{x}_0) \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\,d\mathbf{x}_0 \\
&= -\mathcal{H}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0)\big) + \mathcal{H}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0), p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

The data distribution is fixed, so its entropy $\mathcal{H}(q(\mathbf{x}_0))$ is a constant. Minimizing the KL divergence is therefore the same as minimizing the **cross-entropy**, which is the expected negative log-likelihood. Introducing the intermediate steps $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ as latent variables:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0), p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\big) &= -\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_0)} \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0) \\
&= -\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_0)} \log \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})\,d\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \\
&= -\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_0)} \log \int q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)\,\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}\,d\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \\
&= -\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_0)} \log \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} \left[\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

### Jensen's upper bound

For a convex function $\varphi$, **Jensen's inequality** states:

$$
\varphi\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big) \le \mathbb{E}\big[\varphi(X)\big]
$$

Since $-\log$ is convex, we can move the expectation outside the log. This gives an upper bound on the cross-entropy, the familiar variational bound:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0), p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\big) &\le -\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_0)}\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} \log\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

### Splitting the bound per step

The bound still involves a joint distribution over all $T$ steps. Both processes are Markov chains, so both factor into single steps. One trick does most of the work. For $t \ge 2$, rewrite each forward step with Bayes' theorem,
$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)\,\dfrac{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}$,
so that the extra ratios telescope:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{\prod_{t=1}^{T} q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T) + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T) + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)} + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T) + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T)} + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)}{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)} - \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{L_T} + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \underbrace{-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}_{L_0}\Big]
\end{aligned}
$$

So the cross-entropy is bounded above by a sum of simple per-step terms:

$$
\mathcal{H}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_0), p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\big) \le L_T + L_{T-1} + \dots + L_1 + L_0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_T &= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T)\big) \\
L_{t-1} &= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\big), \qquad 2 \le t \le T \\
L_0 &= -\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

### Terms that matter

$L_T$ has nothing to learn. $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T)$ is a standard Gaussian, and $q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$ depends only on the fixed noise schedule. It is a constant and can be ignored.

$L_0$ is the final decoding step from $\mathbf{x}_1$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$. After training, we sample this step from $\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_0;\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1, 1),\ \sigma_1^2\mathbf{I}\big)$.

$L_{1:T-1}$ is where learning happens. Each term is the KL divergence between two reverse steps. One is the true step $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$, derived above. The other is the model's step $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$.

### The noise-prediction loss

DDPM fixes the model's variance to $\sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}$. When two Gaussians have the same variance, the KL divergence between them reduces to the squared distance between their means:

$$
L_{t-1} = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\big\lVert \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \big\rVert^2\right] + C
$$

Now comes the **reparameterization**. The model's mean takes the same form as the true mean, with the true noise replaced by the predicted noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right)
$$

Substituting both means, everything except the noise terms cancels:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{t-1} - C &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\,\big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \big\rVert^2\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\,\big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\big) \big\rVert^2\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

DDPM goes one step further and drops the weighting factor. The result is the simple objective DDPM is known for: **predict the noise that was added.**

$$
L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\big) \big\rVert^2\right]
$$

### Noise prediction trains best

In the DDPM ablation on unconditional CIFAR-10, predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ with the simplified loss gives the best Inception Score and by far the best FID:

| Objective | IS ↑ | FID ↓ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ prediction, $L$ with learned diagonal $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ | 7.28 ± 0.10 | 23.69 |
| $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ prediction, $L$ with fixed isotropic $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ | 8.06 ± 0.09 | 13.22 |
| $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ prediction, $\lVert \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_\theta \rVert^2$ | unstable | unstable |
| $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ prediction, $L$ with learned diagonal $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ | unstable | unstable |
| $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ prediction, $L$ with fixed isotropic $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ | 7.67 ± 0.13 | 13.51 |
| $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ prediction, $L_{\text{simple}}$ | **9.46 ± 0.11** | **3.17** |

*Higher Inception Score (IS) and lower FID are better. "Unstable" marks runs that failed to train and produced poor samples. Source: Ho et al., 2020, Table 2.*

The paper also gives a theoretical motivation:

> "We have shown that the ε-prediction parameterization both resembles Langevin dynamics and simplifies the diffusion model's variational bound to an objective that resembles denoising score matching." (Ho et al., 2020)

## Training and sampling

With the loss in hand, the full method fits in two short algorithms.

> **Algorithm 1: Training**
>
> 1. **repeat**
> 2. Sample an image $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim q(\mathbf{x}_0)$ from the dataset
> 3. Sample a timestep $t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\{1, \dots, T\})$
> 4. Sample noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
> 5. Take a gradient descent step on $\nabla_\theta \big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\big) \big\rVert^2$
> 6. **until** converged

Because $t$ is sampled uniformly, the network sees every noise level during training. Once trained, it can predict the noise in any $\mathbf{x}_t$, at any step $t$.

> **Algorithm 2: Sampling**
>
> 1. Start from pure noise: $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
> 2. **for** $t = T, \dots, 1$ **do**
> 3. $\quad\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ if $t > 1$, otherwise $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$
> 4. $\quad\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \dfrac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right) + \sigma_t\mathbf{z}$
> 5. **end for**
> 6. **return** $\mathbf{x}_0$

Sampling applies the reverse-process mean derived above one step at a time. The network's predicted noise stands in for the true noise. After $T$ steps, pure noise has become an image from the data distribution.

## Derivation recap

- The forward process has the closed form $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$, so any noise level can be sampled in one step.
- The reverse posterior $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ is Gaussian, with mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$ and variance $\tilde{\sigma}_t^2$.
- Training makes $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ as close as possible to $q(\mathbf{x}_0)$:
  1. Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0))$ is the same as minimizing the cross-entropy $\mathcal{H}(q(\mathbf{x}_0), p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0))$.
  2. Jensen's inequality bounds the cross-entropy from above by $\sum_{t=0}^{T} L_t$, a sum of per-step KL terms.
  3. Each learnable term $L_{t-1}$ compares two Gaussians with the same variance, so it reduces to the distance between their means.
  4. Reparameterizing the mean in terms of the noise turns the loss into a noise-prediction loss, $\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta \rVert^2$.
- Generation starts from pure Gaussian noise and denoises step by step with the trained model.

## DDPM paper results

On 256 × 256 LSUN, DDPM reached FIDs of **7.89** on Church and **4.90** on Bedroom. FID measures how far the generated images are from real ones, so lower is better. The CIFAR-10 scores appear in the ablation table above.

<details>
<summary>Paper setup</summary>

All experiments use $T = 1000$ steps and a variance schedule that increases linearly from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$. Each step stays small enough for the reverse steps to remain close to Gaussian. The schedule still makes $q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$ almost exactly a standard Gaussian, so that $L_T \approx 0$.

The noise predictor $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is a **U-Net**, a convolutional encoder–decoder. Its skip connections carry features from each encoder level straight to the matching decoder level. The timestep $t$ is fed in through a sinusoidal embedding.

### Shared noisy latents

Run the reverse process several times from the *same* partially noised image $\mathbf{x}_t$. From pure noise ($t = 1000$), the samples have almost nothing in common. From lightly noised images ($t = 250$), they keep the original's identity and differ only in the details. The model has learned what is plausible at each level of detail.

## Brain-signal reconstruction

Takagi and Nishimoto (CVPR 2023) use Stable Diffusion to reconstruct what a person is looking at from **fMRI brain signals**. Their method needs no training or fine-tuning of a complex deep-learning model. Once a diffusion model captures a distribution, it can be conditioned on signals as different as text and brain activity.

## Open questions

A trained diffusion model represents an entire data distribution, much as a single linear equation represents every point on a line. Sampling from that distribution is then as easy as feeding Gaussian noise through the model.

Questions I wanted to explore next:

- Does face swapping work by starting the reverse process from a real face image rather than pure noise?
- Can diffusion models fill in missing values, or generate other kinds of data, such as audio or text?
- How can we tell whether a diffusion model generated an image, and what ethical problems will these models raise?
